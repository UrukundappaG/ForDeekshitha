1. Introduction — Background and Context

The management of thermal comfort in Indian urban households represents a deepening crisis where ancient survival needs intersect with modern economic realities. Despite India achieving remarkable economic growth, the fundamental question of how urban residents stay cool during scorching summers remains alarmingly unresolved for the majority of the population. This stagnation in cooling access occurs against a backdrop of rapidly intensifying climate conditions, creating a stark and dangerous contrast between the comfort experienced by India's affluent minority and the heat-stressed reality endured by the vast majority.

India's urban heat crisis is not a future projection — it is a present-day emergency. Rapid urban growth has caused +4–7°C warming in Bangalore alone, driven by the Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect. Over 700 heatwave events over the past five decades have claimed more than 17,000 lives (CNN, 2024). A World Bank report (2022) projects that heat stress could result in 34 million of the projected 80 million global job losses by 2030, disproportionately affecting India's workforce. Young professionals in non-air-conditioned environments suffer a documented 13% performance loss due to heat stress.

The current cooling paradigm operates on a brutal binary: expensive, energy-intensive air conditioners on one end, and wholly inadequate ceiling fans on the other. Only 24% of Indian households own any form of active cooling, and a mere 6–8% use air conditioners. The ₹250 crore conventional air cooler market reaches only 10–12% of households. This leaves the overwhelming majority — over 280 million households — exposed to rising temperatures with no viable solution. Meanwhile, the ₹15,000–₹50,000 upfront cost of an AC, combined with monthly electricity bills that can exceed ₹3,000 in summer, places mechanical cooling permanently out of reach for low-to-middle-income families.

This service design project addresses that gap. TerraTrunk is an innovative, terracotta-based hybrid cooling system developed by Team Gaia at Atria University, Bengaluru, that merges centuries-old evaporative cooling wisdom with modern engineering to deliver effective, affordable, and eco-friendly cooling at a fraction of the energy cost of conventional systems. Operating at just 50W — 90% less than an air conditioner — TerraTrunk provides 4–6°C temperature reduction in a portable, plug-and-play form factor priced accessibly for middle-class urban households.

This document maps the complete service design journey: from the discovery of the urban cooling crisis through user research, to the design and validation of TerraTrunk as a transformative service, culminating in a future-state blueprint for scaling across India's hottest cities.

2. Problem Statement

Problem Significance

The implications of inadequate cooling access extend far beyond individual discomfort to encompass profound societal, economic, and environmental costs. Economic losses from India's cooling gap manifest in multiple compounding ways:

  • Productivity loss: Heat stress causes a documented 13% performance reduction for individuals working without cooling. At scale across India's urban workforce, this represents hundreds of crores in lost GDP annually.
  • Health costs: 700+ heatwave events have claimed 17,000+ lives over five decades. Heat-related illnesses generate enormous healthcare expenditure, disproportionately burdening lower-income families.
  • Energy inequality: The 24% of households that can afford ACs collectively drive 40% of peak residential electricity demand, worsening the energy crisis for all. By 2050, electricity demand from residential AC units in India is projected to exceed the total electricity consumption of the entire African continent today.
  • Environmental injustice: Air conditioners cool indoors but dump heat outside, driving up to 40% of peak power consumption and worsening city-wide UHI temperatures — making life harder for those who cannot afford the very cooling that worsens their conditions.
"When I use an AC, I enjoy cooling comfort… but I also create heat, waste energy, and make life harder for others. If I don't have AC or even a fan, I'm left with no relief from the heat, yet I still face the rising costs and climate impact caused by others' cooling."
— Captured sentiment from field research, 2025

When aggregated, the national economic burden of inadequate affordable cooling — in lost productivity, health costs, and energy inefficiency — reaches catastrophic proportions. Passive cooling measures alone could curb the demand for cooling by 24% by 2050, saving $3 trillion globally and negating greenhouse gas emissions equivalent to 1.3 billion tons of CO₂.

Initial Problem Statement

Based on preliminary observations of the urban cooling landscape in Bangalore and initial rounds of contextual inquiry, our initial problem statement is:

"How might we transform residential cooling from an expensive, energy-intensive privilege into an affordable, sustainable utility service that provides low-to-middle-income urban households with real thermal comfort, without worsening the urban heat island effect or burdening household electricity bills?"

This problem statement was subsequently tested, refined, and crystallised through comprehensive primary research — including quantitative surveys with 239 respondents across two research phases, qualitative interviews to understand emotional dimensions and behavioural patterns, and stakeholder analysis to map ecosystem dynamics. The research either validated this initial hypothesis or revealed deeper systemic issues requiring reframing, ensuring our final problem statement is grounded in empirical evidence.

4. Phase 1: Discover — Research & Understanding

Objective: Develop comprehensive, evidence-based understanding of the current urban cooling service ecosystem, user behaviours, pain points, and systemic failures through primary research and stakeholder analysis.

User Research Component

Quantitative Research — Phase 1 (n=50)

Method: Structured face-to-face surveys with 20 structured questions covering five thematic domains:

  • Demographic profiling and household type
  • Current cooling practices and behaviours
  • Heat stress awareness and experience
  • Willingness-to-pay and price sensitivity
  • Technology adoption readiness and product preferences

Sampling: Middle-class homeowners in Bangalore who do not own an air conditioner or are actively seeking alternatives. Homes ranged from 600–1,200 sq ft (typical middle-income apartment sizes).

Distribution: On-ground visits to residential communities, resident welfare associations, and professional networks.

Quantitative Research — Phase 2 (n=239, Google Survey)

A second, broader survey wave was deployed digitally, collecting 239 responses across living situations (own house, college hostel/PG, rented apartment, community hostel, footpath/informal) and age groups. This phase captured:

  • When respondents feel the most heat indoors (Morning / Afternoon / Evening / Night / All day)
  • Five primary pain points with quantified prevalence
  • Willingness-to-pay thresholds for a portable cooling device
  • Attitudes toward conventional coolers vs. ACs

Qualitative Research

Method: Semi-structured in-depth interviews (30–45 minutes each) and observational sessions in respondents' homes.

Protocol: Four thematic sections exploring: current cooling routines, critical heat-stress incidents, perception of existing solutions, and ideal future states.

Participant Selection: Purposive sampling representing three distinct user archetypes:

  • Budget-conscious urban renter (representative of nuclear/single families in compact flats, high cost sensitivity)
  • Heat-stressed young professional (representative of working singles in PGs/hostels, performance-impacted, tech-open)
  • Space-constrained elderly user (representative of pensioners in small apartments, portability-prioritising, low digital literacy)

6. Root Cause Analysis — 5 Whys Methodology

Urban Indian cooling access operates as a fundamentally broken service ecosystem where the delivery of thermal comfort — a basic human need — has been artificially bifurcated into an expensive, energy-intensive luxury (AC) and an ineffective, free alternative (fan). This is not merely a product gap; it represents a systemic service design failure spanning economic structures, urban infrastructure, regulatory frameworks, and supply chain organisations.

Problem A: Low-to-Middle-Income Users Cannot Access Adequate Cooling

Surface Issue: 86% of households rely on ceiling fans despite temperatures regularly exceeding 40°C

Why 1? Air conditioners are too expensive to purchase and operate for the majority

Why 2? No viable intermediate product exists between an ineffective fan and an expensive AC

Why 3? Market innovation has focused on the premium segment (split ACs, inverter ACs) where margins are higher

Why 4? The assumption that "those who need cooling will eventually be able to afford an AC" has delayed innovation for the mass market

Why 5 — ROOT CAUSE: The cooling market was designed around aspirational aspiration, not universal access. The ₹10-billion industry serves a 24% minority while the 76% majority is treated as a future market, not a present need. No systemic design effort has addressed the actual lived reality of 280+ million households without viable cooling.

Problem B: Conventional Coolers Fail to Meet Urban Household Needs

Surface Issue: 42.2% cite high electricity bills as top pain point; 11.4% abandon coolers due to noise

Why 1? Conventional coolers were designed for large, well-ventilated homes with stable water supply

Why 2? India's urban housing stock has shifted dramatically — average Mumbai apartment is now 735 sq ft; PGs and hostels are smaller

Why 3? Cooler design has not evolved to accommodate compact urban living, rental housing, or noise-sensitive work-from-home contexts

Why 4? Manufacturers lack real-time feedback loops from users in these contexts; retail channels do not surface these pain points

Why 5 — ROOT CAUSE: Cooling product design operates on outdated assumptions about how Indians live. The shift to compact, rented, acoustically sensitive urban environments has not been matched by product evolution. The market's dominant paradigm — big, loud, high-water-consumption evaporative cooling — is fundamentally misaligned with the realities of 118 million middle-class urban households.

Problem C: The Urban Heat Island Effect Creates a Vicious Cycle

Surface Issue: Urban areas are 4–7°C hotter than surrounding rural areas; heat demand rises as cities expand

Why 1? More people buying ACs creates more outdoor heat rejection, raising ambient temperature

Why 2? Higher ambient temperature drives more AC purchases — a self-reinforcing loop

Why 3? Conventional cooling infrastructure has no mechanism to avoid contributing to UHI

Why 4? Energy grid constraints intensify during peak cooling demand, causing brownouts that reduce cooling effectiveness for all

Why 5 — ROOT CAUSE: The dominant cooling technology (compression-based refrigerant AC) is architecturally incompatible with urban density at scale. The more widespread adoption becomes, the worse the problem becomes for the collective. A fundamentally different technology class — one that cools without rejecting heat externally — is required for sustainable urban thermal comfort.

8. Refined Problem Statement

The convergent analysis of behavioural patterns, dual-phase survey results (n=289 combined), and the 5 Whys analysis highlights three fundamental market failures:

  1. Design Gap by Exclusion: The ₹10-billion cooling market has been designed for the 24% who can afford ACs, systematically ignoring the viable middle ground between a ₹500 fan and a ₹35,000 AC where 280 million households actually live.
  2. Technology-Context Misalignment: Existing mid-range cooling solutions (conventional coolers) were designed for large, well-ventilated homes with stable water supply — not the compact, rental, noise-sensitive, frequently-moved urban apartments where the majority of India's growing middle class resides.
  3. Systemic Sustainability Failure: The dominant cooling technology (compression refrigerant AC) worsens the urban heat island it is meant to counter, creating a self-defeating cycle that disproportionately harms those who cannot participate in it.
Refined Problem Statement:

"How might we redesign the urban residential cooling service to provide affordable, effective, compact, and sustainable thermal comfort for India's 280+ million uncooled households — transforming the current 'all-or-nothing' cooling paradigm into an inclusive, retrofittable service where evaporative cooling, smart design, and local craftsmanship converge to deliver real relief without worsening the urban heat crisis for others?"

This problem statement establishes the foundation for TerraTrunk's comprehensive service redesign spanning: user-facing touchpoints (the device, setup experience, maintenance service), delivery interactions (e-commerce, retail, B2B), backstage operations (artisan supply chain, production, logistics), and support infrastructure (IoT sensing, cloud monitoring, community ecosystem integration).

9. Current State Service Blueprint

Before designing TerraTrunk's interventions, we first comprehensively map how the current residential cooling service actually operates — not how it is supposed to work, but how it functions in ground reality across millions of daily user interactions with fans, coolers, and the absence of cooling.

Stage 1: Awareness — User Becomes Conscious of Heat Problem

Physical Evidence

  • The Ceiling Fan (The False Comfort): Present in 90%+ of Indian homes, the fan is the primary interaction point with "cooling." At ambient temperatures above 35°C, a fan merely circulates hot air, raising perceived temperature. Users have no visual indicator of its ineffectiveness beyond subjective discomfort.
  • The Urban Apartment: Average 1 BHK in Bangalore is 600–900 sq ft. West-facing or top-floor apartments become heat traps by 2 PM. Concrete buildings retain heat well into the evening. Users experience a completely sealed "heat box" with no passive cooling strategy.
  • The Body (Primary Thermometer): Sweating, difficulty concentrating, irritability, and lethargy are the only "indicators" available to users. There is no device measuring indoor temperature or heat stress level. The body itself becomes the detection mechanism.
  • Scattered Digital Artifacts: Google search history for "best cooler under ₹5000," WhatsApp messages to family about heat, electricity bill PDFs showing summer spikes — these scattered records create retrieval friction without coherent service information.

Customer Actions

  • Primitive Mitigation Attempts: Wetting a towel and placing it on the neck; closing curtains; using a spray bottle; sitting on the floor (cooler due to concrete conduction). These represent improvised, non-scalable workarounds with no systematic relief.
  • Fan Position Obsession: Users adjust fan speed, direction, and personal positioning multiple times daily trying to optimise airflow. This effort produces diminishing returns as ambient temperatures rise, and consumes mental energy without solving the core problem.
  • Escape Behaviour: Going to malls, libraries, or cafes in peak heat hours. This behaviour — effectively outsourcing thermal comfort to commercial spaces — represents a significant economic and time cost that is invisible in cooling market analyses.
  • Anxiety Cycle: Heat-stressed users report increased irritability, difficulty sleeping, reduced appetite, and inability to focus. The psychological toll of sustained heat exposure without relief creates chronic low-grade stress that affects productivity, relationships, and wellbeing.

Frontstage Interactions

[NO COMMERCIAL SERVICE INTERACTION AT AWARENESS STAGE] Users struggle alone with heat, primitive workarounds, and escalating discomfort. No proactive outreach from cooling brands, no alerts, no community support mechanisms.

Backstage — Service Provider Inaction

Cooling brands have no real-time awareness of when users are experiencing heat stress. Backend remains idle, waiting for user-initiated purchase intent before any interaction begins. The party most affected (user) has no information; the party with most insight (manufacturer) remains passive.

Stage 2: Consideration — User Researches Cooling Solutions

Physical Evidence

  • Retail store visits (Croma, local electronics shops): overwhelmed by AC-dominated displays; conventional coolers are bulky, noisy, often unavailable for test
  • E-commerce tabs open across multiple devices; comparison tools that compare AC to AC but not the middle ground
  • Peer consultations via WhatsApp groups, apartment RWAs: "What cooler do you use? Does it actually work in a small room?"

Customer Actions

  • The Research Dead-End: Users searching for "affordable cooling" find either fans (insufficient) or ACs (unaffordable). Conventional coolers surface as the middle option, but user reviews consistently flag noise, space, and humidity issues. Users enter a decision paralysis state: "Nothing seems right for my situation."
  • Price Anchor Shock: Seeing AC prices (₹25,000–₹50,000) creates an anchor that makes all alternatives feel like compromises. Users simultaneously can't afford ACs and don't trust cheaper alternatives — caught between two unsatisfactory options.
  • Social Research: Asking neighbours and colleagues who use coolers. Most report mixed experiences; many have abandoned their coolers. This peer research reinforces hesitation rather than providing clear direction.
  • Extended Decision Paralysis: Repeat visits to the same e-commerce pages over days or weeks without purchasing. Users bookmark products, add to cart, and abandon — performing research behaviours without reaching a confident decision because no option clearly fits their context.

Frontstage Interactions

  • Retail staff (if consulted): typically push higher-margin ACs; limited knowledge of mid-range evaporative solutions; no personalised advice for small-room/rental scenarios
  • Online chat bots: generic responses; unable to advise on suitability for specific room sizes or rental restrictions
  • No proactive brand engagement: no SMS, email, or app-based guidance based on seasonal temperature data or user profile

Stage 3: Decision — User Makes a Forced Compromise

Customer Actions

  • The Compromise Purchase: Most users either (a) buy a conventional cooler despite doubts about noise/size/humidity, or (b) buy a more powerful fan as a false compromise, or (c) give up and endure the heat. None of these outcomes represent a satisfactory resolution.
  • The Renter's Paralysis: Renters face an additional constraint: they cannot install AC and may not want to invest in a bulky cooler they'll need to transport when they move. This paralyses the decision entirely for a large segment of the population.
  • Cognitive Dissonance at Purchase: Users who buy conventional coolers report second-guessing immediately: "Is the noise really that bad? Will it actually cool the room? Did I just waste money?" No reassurance mechanism exists post-purchase except actually living with the product.

Backstage — Service Provider Opportunity Missed

At the decision moment, brands could provide (but don't): room-size compatibility calculators, noise level comparisons for small rooms, rental-home suitability certification, first-purchase trial periods, or community reviews from similar household profiles. The absence of these tools pushes users toward safer but inadequate choices (buying nothing or buying a fan).

Stage 4: Experience — Living with Current Cooling Reality

Physical Evidence

  • Conventional cooler filling water tank daily (10–15 litres) — water spillage on floor, mineral residue buildup, algae growth
  • Filter cleaning required every 2–4 weeks — wet, messy process; many users abandon maintenance, reducing effectiveness
  • Cooler dominates a corner of the room (2–3 sq ft of floor space in a 90 sq ft room)
  • Constant background noise (55–70 dB) that makes video calls, study, and sleep difficult
  • High electricity bill at end of month: cooler uses 150–250W vs. fan's 60–100W

Customer Actions

  • Cooler Performance Anxiety: Users adjust water levels, pad wetness, fan speed, and room ventilation trying to optimise a product that fundamentally doesn't fit their context. This daily tinkering is frustrating and time-consuming.
  • Noise Avoidance Behaviours: Turning cooler off during calls; choosing not to run cooler during study sessions despite heat; waking to turn it off at night; using it only when "the heat is unbearable" — all of which negate its cooling benefit at precisely the moments it's most needed.
  • The Abandoned Cooler: Multiple survey participants reported coolers sitting unused in a corner — purchased with hope, abandoned within a season. The product becomes a monument to an unmet need.
  • Electricity Bill Shock: Users who run coolers through summer report 30–50% increase in electricity bills. This directly contradicts the mental model of "cooler as affordable AC alternative" and triggers buyer's remorse.

Service Failure Points — Current State Summary

  • Complete absence of post-purchase support: no maintenance service, no optimisation guidance, no performance tracking
  • No feedback loop from users to manufacturers: abandoned coolers generate no signal that reaches the brand
  • No community support network: users struggle with the same problems in isolated parallel, with no shared knowledge base
  • No upgrade path: users who outgrow a product or whose needs change have no continuity of service — they start from scratch

10. Phase 3: Develop — Prototype & Iteration

The TerraTrunk Solution — Design Philosophy

TerraTrunk responds to every identified service failure with a deliberate design decision. It is not merely a product — it is a service system that transforms the cooling experience from a crisis-driven, high-friction, high-cost ordeal into a seamless, sustainable, aesthetically pleasing service touchpoint.

Core Principle: TerraTrunk leverages terracotta's millennia-old evaporative cooling properties, enhanced by a precision dual-fan airflow system and closed-loop water circulation, to deliver 4–6°C temperature reduction at just 50W — achieving what 90% less energy, 100% less refrigerant, and 100% more portability than a split AC.

Hardware Development — Iterative Prototyping

Iteration 1 — Initial Prototype

Initial tests were conducted with a smaller terracotta pot, two fans at different positions, and four DHT11 sensors (one control room, one mid-room, two near fans). A baseline test with a standard pedestal fan confirmed no temperature change from airflow alone, validating evaporative cooling as the sole mechanism. Results: 3°C temperature reduction at product output, but no meaningful room-level temperature change. Learnings: insufficient airflow and evaporation rate; fan CFM may be too high, evacuating cooled air before it diffuses; pot surface area insufficient.

Iteration 2 — Refined Prototype (Final MVP)

Based on Ambiator's guidance, the design was refined: replaced dual axial fans with a single, more powerful centrifugal fan; introduced cellulose paper as a denser evaporative medium alongside terracotta beads; implemented a dual water pump system (total <5W) for even wall and bead distribution; upgraded to DHT-II sensors with ESP32 cloud logging for real-time monitoring.

Testing Protocol — IMAC Adaptive Comfort Framework

Performance was evaluated using the Indian Model for Adaptive Comfort (IMAC). With an outdoor running mean temperature of 26°C:

Neutral Comfort Temperature = 0.28 × 26 + 17.8 = 25.08°C

Acceptable comfort ranges for naturally ventilated buildings: 22.6°C – 27.6°C

Testing setup: Two identical rooms in a three-story building, Bangalore. Control room (2nd floor, passive), Test room (1st floor, with TerraTrunk). ESP32 cloud logging continuously captured temperature and humidity at intake (Chamber 1), output (Chamber 2), and 1 metre from output across 24-hour periods.

Key Testing Result: Product output temperature remained consistently 3.5–4°C cooler than product input across the 24-hour cycle, demonstrating sustained evaporative cooling. Output humidity averaged 12–16% higher than control room, beneficial in Bangalore's dry afternoon conditions (April–May). The cooling effect was confirmed as evaporative cooling — not mere airflow — as baseline pedestal fan tests showed zero temperature change.

Phase 3 — Long-Term (6 Months – 3 Years): Transform & Lead

  • Community Ecosystem: "Cooling Guardians" platform — a peer community of TerraTrunk users sharing cooling tips, summer survival strategies, and co-creating next-generation features through participatory design
  • Artisan Network at Scale: Project Chittoor partnership scaled to 600 villages; TerraTrunk becomes a revenue source for rural pottery artisans, creating a circular economy linking urban cooling demand with rural craft supply
